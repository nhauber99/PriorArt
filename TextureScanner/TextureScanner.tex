	\section{Abstract}
	Photometric stereo is a technique used to extract three-dimensional (3D) shape information of various materials and examine how these materials interact with light. This document serves as a defensive publication for a product designed to scan materials, analyzing their 3D shape, and their reflection, transmission, absorption, scattering, polarization, fluorescence, phosphorescence, thermal emission, or other optical and radiometric behavior. A key function of the product is to determine the parameters of a Spatially Varying Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function (SVBRDF), Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function (BRDF), Bidirectional Transmittance Distribution Function (BTDF), Bidirectional Scattering Distribution Function (BSDF), Bidirectional Surface Scattering Reflectance Distribution Function (BSSRDF), phase function, emission function, or another spatially varying material model suitable for measurement, rendering, simulation, quality control, machine vision, or manufacturing.
	
	The materials for scanning can be anything that manipulates light or radiation in some way, including but not limited to hot steel slabs, liquids, fabrics, leather, plants, rocks, skin, hair, photoluminescent materials, paper, wood, veneers, plastics, resin, glass, ceramics, metals, paints, coatings, semiconductor surfaces, electronic displays, printed materials, composite materials, food, biological samples, cultural heritage objects, automotive materials, fashion materials, packaging materials, transparent films, translucent films, perforated materials, and multilayer materials. The scanner deploys diverse light forms, each varying in form, direction, polarization, modulation, coherence, time profile, and/or spectral distribution, to illuminate the material. Utilizing a range of light sources mitigates limitations that would be present if relying solely on a single light type. \cite{SURVEY}
	
	A particularly important embodiment is a scanner that combines front-side reflection capture with a back-side polarized transmission capture. The back-side capture can be performed in multiple spectral bands and multiple analyzer polarization states to separate a polarization-preserving direct-transmission or transparency component from a depolarized scattering or translucency component. These transmission maps can be spatially co-registered with reflection-derived maps, such as albedo, normal, height, roughness, specular, anisotropy, coat, fuzz, sheen, metalness, index-of-refraction, and other BRDF or SVBRDF parameters, and converted into a renderable material definition such as Disney BRDF, OpenPBR Surface, Autodesk Standard Surface, Adobe Standard Material, MaterialX, USD/MaterialX, MDL, glTF, Unreal Engine, Unity, Blender, or another physically based or artist-oriented material representation. \cite{DISNEY_BRDF,OPENPBR,MATERIALX}
	
	More information on the current prototype can be found here:\\
	\href{https://www.photometric.io/blog/material-scanner/}{https://www.photometric.io/blog/material-scanner/}
	
	A solid introduction to photometric stereo can be found in this reference: \cite{SURVEY}

	\section{Description}
	The scanner comprises several optional components, including:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item One or more cameras that capture the light's interaction with the material. The use of multiple cameras enhances data availability and allows for a more accurate 3D model of the object through stereophotogrammetry. By integrating high-frequency detail provided by the normal map derived from photometric stereo, an enhanced 3D representation of the object can be achieved. The cameras can be fitted with a color filter array (CFA), be monochrome, be paired with a filter wheel, use a Bayer pattern, use a multi-spectral filter mosaic, use a color-polarization filter array, use a division-of-focal-plane polarization sensor, use a beam splitter feeding several sensors, or use a tunable optical filter.
		
		\item Broadband point light sources, such as white LEDs, are typically used due to their affordability and ease of mathematical modeling. Despite these benefits, they may not perform well with glossy materials with low roughness, and hard shadows can cause artifacts unless properly managed. This can be mitigated by using them in greater number, by moving them, by diffusing them, by combining them with area lights, or by processing shadowed pixels as missing or low-confidence observations.
		
		\item Colored or spectrally variable point light sources, such as colored LEDs, lasers, laser diodes, supercontinuum sources, xenon flash lamps, broadband light sources with filters, diffraction-grating sources, acousto-optic tunable filters, liquid-crystal tunable filters, quantum-dot light sources, phosphor-converted LEDs, or narrow-band interference-filter systems, can provide useful information about color, absorption, photoluminescence, fluorescence, phosphorescence, metamerism, wavelength-dependent scattering, and wavelength-dependent polarization behavior of the material. An inexpensive yet effective method to produce colored light involves collimating broadband light and diffracting it using a diffraction grating, causing different wavelengths to emerge at different angles. This light can be focused onto a controllable optical slit. By varying the position and opening size of the slit, one can select a specific wavelength band. Ideally this light is then diffused via a normal diffuser or integrating sphere. See figure \ref{variable_light_source} for a drawing.
		
		\item Linear light sources, either broadband/white or colored/spectrally variable, can significantly improve the quality of scans for highly glossy materials. \cite{LINEAR_LIGHT} Linear sources can be implemented by LED strips, laser lines, cylindrical lenses, fiber optics, scanning slits, linear OLEDs, LCD panels, DLP projections, or by integrating multiple point sources behind a diffuser.
		
		\item Area light sources can replace point light sources to aid with materials exhibiting low roughness. \cite{PS_AreaLights} A potentially more effective, albeit challenging, alternative could be to use point light sources behind a medium that scatters their light. This would result in lights having a falloff and could overlap each other. This means that any point on the hemisphere is covered by at least one light by some degree. If a glossy surface is illuminated by three lights with the same amount then the normal lies between those. This would allow scanning highly specular objects. See figure \ref{blurred_area_lights} for a drawing.
		
		\item One or more area light sources can be placed behind the material to measure translucency, opacity, transparency, transmission color, transmission roughness, diffuse transmission, direct transmission, haze, scattering, subsurface transport, perforations, porosity, thickness variation, edge leakage, caustic transmission, and spectral absorption.
		
		\item All light sources can be paired with linear, circular, elliptical, radial, spatially varying, electrically switchable, mechanically rotatable, or otherwise controllable polarizers to discern specular reflection, diffuse reflection, polarization-preserving reflection, depolarized reflection, direct transmission, and depolarized translucency. Translucency and transparency can be separated using polarizers, because light waves directly passing through the material may maintain polarization while scattered waves alter, randomize, rotate, ellipticize, or otherwise modify their polarization state.
		
		\item Other 3D scanning technologies, such as structured light scanners, stereo cameras, stereophotogrammetry, multi-view stereo, depth-from-defocus, focus stacking, shape from polarization, time-of-flight cameras, laser triangulation, lidar, confocal scanning, optical coherence tomography, silhouette scanning, turntable scanning, robot-arm scanning, computed tomography, ultrasound, or combinations thereof, can be used to capture or assist a 3D representation of the material or object.
		
		\item An optical rangefinder, autofocus system, depth sensor, phase-detection autofocus sensor, contrast autofocus algorithm, dual-pixel autofocus sensor, focus sweep, or learned focus estimator can help adjust the focus of the camera lenses electronically or manually.
		
		\item Special lenses, such as tilt-shift lenses, macro lenses, telecentric lenses, microscope objectives, endoscopic lenses, zoom lenses, anamorphic lenses, liquid lenses, electrically tunable lenses, polarization-preserving lenses, UV lenses, IR lenses, or lens arrays, can be employed for scanning flat, curved, microscopic, large, or difficult-to-access materials.
		
		\item To minimize stray light, the device's interior can be coated with a material that absorbs light well, such as black velvet, flocking, Vantablack-like coatings, black anodized metal, baffles, honeycombs, matte paint, beam dumps, or light traps. The scanner can be shielded by a cover or enclosure to prevent the influence of ambient light. Alternatively, the scanning process can be performed in a dark environment, or ambient light can be measured and subtracted using light-off frames or temporally modulated illumination.
		
		\item The light sources and cameras can be moved around the material, either physically or by using mirrors, galvanometers, prisms, optical fibers, beam splitters, relay optics, scanning stages, robot arms, drones, or other optical or mechanical elements. Alternatively, the material itself can be moved while the light sources and cameras stay stationary. For instance, the material can be placed on a rotating platform, conveyor belt, XY stage, vacuum table, gantry, robotic end effector, microscope slide stage, transparent platen, stretching frame, tensioning frame, or tiltable rotary table.
		
		\item A mobile device may be constructed by attaching the light sources to the multiple legs of the device, akin to a multi-legged tripod. A camera would be centrally mounted, facing downwards, and additional side-mounted cameras can be added to provide multiple viewing angles. This design would be both flexible and portable, with collapsible legs for ease of storage and transport. The mobile device may also use a smartphone, tablet, AR headset, wearable camera, handheld scanner, flashlight attachment, ring light, clip-on polarizer, or foldable light tent.

	\section{Methodology}
	The proposed scanning process can involve the following steps, in any suitable order, with any subset repeated, interleaved, skipped, or replaced by an equivalent step:
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{Calibration:} Calibration is performed to ascertain the camera's intrinsic properties, such as focal length, principal point, vignetting, distortion parameters, spectral response, black level, read noise, gain, gamma or linearization curve, rolling-shutter timing, sensor non-uniformity, polarization sensitivity, color correction matrix, and focus behavior. Calibration also includes extrinsic properties, such as position and orientation in relation to the material, light sources, robot axes, turntables, screens, backlights, and reference coordinate systems. The position, angular distribution, spatial brightness distribution, spectral distribution, polarization state, temporal profile, thermal behavior, and stability of the light sources are also calibrated.
		
		\item \textbf{Sample preparation and positioning:} The material can be placed flat, curved, stretched, relaxed, clamped, vacuum-held, index-matched, immersed, tensioned, heated, cooled, humidified, dried, compressed, or otherwise prepared. Fiducials, calibration dots, QR codes, AprilTags, ArUco markers, corners, pins, printed borders, transparent registration marks, or natural texture features may be used to register scans.
		
		\item \textbf{Illumination and capturing:} The material is illuminated by the light sources, and the cameras capture the resulting interaction between light and material. In a simple setup each image captures the object illuminated by a single light. Optionally, more images per light can be captured with varying cross or parallel polarization, varying analyzer angle, varying source polarization, different band-pass filters in front of the camera and/or lights, different wavelengths, different exposure times, different focus distances, different apertures, or different temporal delays. This process can be repeated at different focus points to allow focus stacking, at different camera positions to allow multi-view capture, at different sample poses to improve angular coverage, and at different environmental states to measure variation.
		
		\item \textbf{Reflection capture:} A front-side reflection sequence can be captured using point lights, area lights, gradient illumination, display illumination, polarized illumination, structured light, or a combination thereof. The reflection sequence can be used to determine normals, height, diffuse albedo, specular albedo, roughness, anisotropy, clearcoat, sheen, fuzz, metalness, IOR, Fresnel parameters, thin-film interference, iridescence, glitter, flakes, diffraction, retroreflection, or other reflectance parameters.
		
		\item \textbf{Transmission capture:} A back-side illumination sequence can be captured using polarized and/or unpolarized broadband, RGB, multispectral, hyperspectral, structured, patterned, collimated, diffuse, directional, telecentric, or time-modulated illumination. The transmitted image can be captured with one or more analyzer polarization states, spectral bands, exposure times, focus distances, or camera positions. This produces maps of direct transparency, opacity, diffuse transmission, depolarized translucency, haze, thickness, absorption, scattering, wavelength-dependent transmission, and polarization-dependent transmission.
		
		\item \textbf{Emission and excitation capture:} For emissive, fluorescent, phosphorescent, thermally emitting, luminescent, or photoluminescent materials, the scanner can illuminate the sample with one wavelength or band and capture emission at another wavelength or band, possibly after a delay. The excitation-emission response can be mapped spatially and used for rendering, material classification, authentication, quality control, and defect detection.
		
		\item \textbf{Post-processing:} The captured images are processed to extract material properties. The algorithms used depend on light sources, sensors, calibrations, and material properties. The outcome can include a normal map, height map, displacement map, opacity map, transparency map, translucency map, thickness map, spectral albedo, SVBRDF parameters, BSSRDF parameters, BTDF parameters, BSDF parameters, fluorescence maps, defect maps, uncertainty maps, a 3D model, a texture atlas, a neural material representation, or a renderable asset.
		
		\item \textbf{Result analysis and validation:} The processed data can be analyzed, visualized, compared against reference materials, rendered under novel lighting, viewed in augmented reality, used in quality control, used for material design, used for computer graphics rendering, used for reverse engineering, used for database search, used for authentication, or used for process control.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	\section{Capture and Processing Details}
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item The scanning process involves capturing images of the reflection, transmission, absorption, scattering, or emission of light from one or more light sources. A group of cameras can simultaneously capture these interactions. The light sources can be modulated to produce a pattern, gradient, pulse, code, polarization state, spectrum, spatial light field, structured-light pattern, or temporal signature in the reflected or transmitted light. If there are multiple cameras and multiple light sources, the maximum total number of images taken for a single scan can equal the number of cameras times the number of light states times the number of wavelengths times the number of polarization states times the number of exposures times the number of focus states times the number of sample poses, but a practical system can capture any reduced subset and infer missing observations.
		
		\item The images captured are processed by a computer, embedded processor, microcontroller, FPGA, GPU, TPU, NPU, cloud service, edge device, phone, tablet, camera firmware, or distributed network. Computations can be executed on the CPU, GPU, FPGA, neural accelerator, or any combination thereof.
		
		\item The images may be transmitted to a computer directly from the cameras, streamed over USB, Ethernet, Camera Link, CoaXPress, Wi-Fi, PCIe, MIPI, or another bus, or stored within the camera and then transferred. Metadata including time, temperature, exposure, current, voltage, polarizer angle, wavelength, stage pose, robot pose, focus, aperture, and calibration version can be stored with each image.
		
		\item Light sources can be toggled on and off using MOSFETs paired with daisy-chained shift registers controlled by a microcontroller or computer. Other forms of communication, such as I2C, SPI, CAN, RS-485, Ethernet, DMX, DALI, PWM, analog control, or direct LED-driver control, are possible. One dimmable constant-current controller for each LED allows multiple dimming methods such as analog, PWM, and shunt FET dimming. The dimming method can be dynamically chosen via software, and may be optimized to reduce flicker, heat, rolling-shutter artifacts, or spectral shifts.
		
		\item Multispectral images can be acquired using multiple colored light sources or different filters. High color accuracy can be achieved by multiplying acquired samples per pixel with a pre-calculated matrix, a spectral reconstruction basis, a learned color conversion, a regularized inverse, or a perceptually optimized transform. See figure \ref{multispectral_to_rgb}. The same principle can be used for reflection color, transmission color, fluorescence color, metamerism prediction, spectral rendering, textile matching, paint matching, and color quality control.
		
		\item Normals of the material can be calculated using images of specular reflection. By comparing these normals with normals calculated from diffuse reflection of broadband or colored light sources, subsurface scattering parameters can be estimated. Once specular normals are obtained, solving for subsurface scattering parameters directly, for example via gradient descent, differentiable rendering, neural inference, or probabilistic inference, can be executed. \cite{SSS}
		
		\item Diffuse and specular reflection may be separated using cross and parallel polarization. In one simple idealization, a crossed-polarized capture $C$ measures diffuse or depolarized reflection $D$, and a parallel capture $P$ measures $D + 2S$, where $S$ is a specular component after appropriate scaling and calibration. Other linear systems, Stokes-vector estimates, Mueller-matrix estimates, robust estimates, multi-angle estimates, learned estimates, or physically based polarization BRDF models can be substituted.
		
		\item In low-signal, saturated, shadowed, highly specular, highly translucent, anisotropic, iridescent, or otherwise difficult regions, the solver can use confidence weights, robust loss functions, interpolation, inpainting, learned priors, local patch similarity, material-class priors, neighboring wavelengths, neighboring polarization states, neighboring views, or additional captures.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	The above details shed light on the in-depth process of capturing and processing images for material scanning. The scanner design, coupled with this comprehensive methodology, allows for accurate capture and analysis of a wide range of material properties.

	\section{Polarized Backlight Capture and Transmission Decomposition}
	One important family of embodiments uses a polarized backlight and a polarization analyzer at the camera to decompose transmitted light into a direct or polarization-preserving component and a depolarized or scattered component. This may be used for fabrics, paper, leaves, skin, wax, stone, wood veneer, plastics, resins, frosted glass, perforated materials, lace, multilayer films, liquid samples, semi-transparent coatings, and any material that transmits or scatters light.
	
	Let $I_{\parallel,\lambda}(x)$ be the observed intensity at image location $x$ for spectral band $\lambda$ when the analyzer is parallel to the source polarization. Let $I_{\perp,\lambda}(x)$ be the observed intensity when the analyzer is crossed. In an idealized two-component model,
	\begin{align}
		I_{\parallel,\lambda}(x) &= T_{\mathrm{pol},\lambda}(x) + \frac{1}{2}T_{\mathrm{dep},\lambda}(x) + B_{\parallel,\lambda}(x),\\
		I_{\perp,\lambda}(x) &= \frac{1}{2}T_{\mathrm{dep},\lambda}(x) + B_{\perp,\lambda}(x),
	\end{align}
	where $T_{\mathrm{pol},\lambda}$ is a polarization-preserving or direct transmission term, $T_{\mathrm{dep},\lambda}$ is a depolarized diffuse transmission or translucency term, and $B$ represents black level, stray light, leakage, extinction-ratio error, and other offsets. After calibration, one possible estimate is
	\begin{align}
		T_{\mathrm{pol},\lambda}(x) &\approx \max(I_{\parallel,\lambda}(x) - I_{\perp,\lambda}(x), 0),\\
		T_{\mathrm{dep},\lambda}(x) &\approx 2 I_{\perp,\lambda}(x).
	\end{align}
	Equivalently, a calibrated matrix model can be solved per pixel, per material class, per wavelength, or globally to account for non-ideal polarizers, lens polarization, camera polarization response, birefringence, partial polarization, multiple scattering, reflections between sample and supports, and leakage through the optical system.
	
	A Stokes-vector implementation can capture three or more analyzer states and compute $S_0$, $S_1$, $S_2$, optionally $S_3$, the angle of polarization, and degree of polarization. One possible decomposition is to treat $S_0 \cdot \mathrm{DoLP}$ as a polarization-preserving component and $S_0 \cdot (1-\mathrm{DoLP})$ as a depolarized component after calibration. A Mueller-matrix implementation can estimate per-pixel or per-region transmission Mueller matrices, enabling maps of depolarization, retardance, diattenuation, birefringence, stress, fiber direction, or anisotropic scattering.
	
	The backlight may be uniform, spatially patterned, angularly varying, collimated, diffuse, telecentric, focused, defocused, time-coded, spectrally coded, or polarization-coded. Multiple backlight directions or angular distributions can be used to estimate a BTDF or transmission lobe. A collimated backlight can emphasize direct transmission. A diffuse backlight can emphasize hemispherical transmission. A structured backlight can detect blur, scattering radius, internal diffusion, optical thickness, lensing, caustics, distortions, and local refractive index variation. A coded or patterned backlight can estimate a point-spread function or scattering kernel for each pixel or region.
	
	The transmission maps can be co-registered with reflection-derived material maps using a shared camera and sample pose, structured-light geometry, fiducials, feature matching, optical flow, non-rigid registration, texture matching, height maps, normal maps, thin-plate splines, learned registration, or a known calibration between front-side and back-side captures. The final output can include direct-transmission color, opacity, alpha, thickness, absorption color, transmittance, haze, transmission roughness, translucency, subsurface color, scattering distance, transmission scatter, or any shader-channel approximation of such quantities.

	\section{Additional Embodiments and Extensions}
	While the current focus is on the primary objectives of the project, there are several potential enhancements and additional applications that could be explored in the future:
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item \textbf{Portability:} Minimizing the weight of the scanner could transform it into a handheld device, offering enhanced convenience and portability. This could broaden its usage scenarios, such as field studies or on-site analysis. A portable version could be realized as a phone accessory, tablet accessory, clip-on polarized light, foldable light tent, handheld dome, tripod device, or robot-mounted probe.
		
		\item \textbf{Advanced Imaging:} The acquisition and separation of multiple reflections from various light sources in a single image could be achieved by using cameras fitted with polarization or color filters. This could involve employing a filter wheel or Color Filter Array (CFA). Having multiple polarized and/or colored light sources in different positions would enhance this setup. A beam splitter could be utilized for efficiency, allowing multiple cameras to capture images from the same perspective. The characteristics of the beam splitter could be adjusted based on the polarization or color of the light, possibly utilizing thin-film filters.
		
		\item \textbf{Digital Material Conversion:} The algorithm used for processing the acquired data could be adapted to convert existing digital materials into other formats. This could be achieved by rendering these materials in a virtualized version of the scanner setup. For instance, the properties of a Disney BRDF, OpenPBR, MaterialX, USD, MDL, game-engine material, or proprietary material could be calculated and converted even if the digital version is in another format.
		
		\item \textbf{GAN, Diffusion, and Neural Network Enhancement:} A Generative Adversarial Network (GAN), diffusion model, transformer, U-Net, neural field, or other learned model could be trained on the resulting textures to improve perception, recover missing details, denoise maps, super-resolve maps, predict material parameters from fewer images, or generate plausible variants. Ideally, the perceptual loss would be calculated on rendered images, which would require gradient backpropagation through the renderer. This could be achieved directly, through analytical approximations, via differentiable rendering, or via trained neural networks to attain a good approximation.
		
		\item \textbf{Simplified Scanner:} A simpler scanner design with fewer light sources and potentially without polarization could be realized. To handle the reduced data, a neural network like a U-Net could be trained to predict different textures. The network training could ideally be done using the results from a more complex scanner as ground truth. The input during training could be renders of the ground truth or a subset of images captured by the larger scanner. The software could also be used as its own product. This could especially be useful for existing scanners that employ photometric stereo in addition to photogrammetry and normally have fewer light sources.
		
		\item \textbf{Data Interpolation for Glossy Materials:} For highly glossy materials, normals and roughness values can be interpolated for pixels that did not contain enough reflected light to calculate the textures accurately. This can be done by setting a minimum specularity ($F_0$) for the material and checking if the measured values align with a high enough roughness value that sufficiently illuminates the pixels. If a lower roughness value is necessary for the measured results, interpolation might be necessary. Once the areas for interpolation are identified, this can be done by examining the nearest patches of normals that did reflect enough light in any image for accurate calculation, or by using a learned prior.
		
		\item \textbf{Augmented Reality Visualization:} Captured materials can be compared to real samples using AR goggles or mixed-reality displays to display a rendered version of the material next to the real one. For this, an approximation of the environmental lighting is necessary, which can be assumed fixed, estimated via machine learning, measured using one or more cameras, or measured using a light probe. The system can iteratively adjust material maps so the rendered material matches the physical sample as observed by a camera.
		
		\item \textbf{Joint Photogrammetry / Photometric Stereo Capture:} By photographing an object illuminated by multiple light sources from multiple directions, one has the data to do a photogrammetry workflow followed by calculating material properties via photometric stereo for each camera angle and mapping those results onto the 3D object. Another approach would be to first apply the photometric stereo step to get a set of material properties for each pixel and camera position. By aggregating all textures to a single multi-channel image, more robust features can be extracted for a photogrammetry workflow. This photogrammetry workflow could be similar to the one used in Meshroom (\href{https://alicevision.org/\#photogrammetry}{https://alicevision.org/\#photogrammetry}), with the main difference that the input to the whole pipeline is a multi-channel texture describing material properties obtained by photometric stereo instead of normal photographs. An additional possible improvement could be to use previously calculated normal maps and/or height maps to aid depth-map estimation, which could improve high-frequency details in the 3D object. Each step can be implemented by analytical methods, heuristics, or neural networks.
		
		\item \textbf{Single-Shot Spectral and Polarization-Multiplexed Photometric Stereo:} Using dual-pixel, quad-pixel, multi-aperture, color-polarization mosaic, beam-splitter, or coded-aperture sensors, one can use spectral multiplexing in combination with polarization multiplexing. For example, two polarization filters with orthogonal polarization may be placed over different halves of a lens or over different sensor pixels. Multiple colored light sources with different spectra and polarizations can then be demultiplexed from a single image or a small number of images. This can work especially well for metallic objects, because they do not have a diffuse reflectance in the same way as dielectrics and cross polarization can strongly suppress reflected light while parallel polarization admits it.
		
		\item \textbf{Active Acquisition:} The scanner can adaptively choose the next capture based on the current estimate and its uncertainty. It may choose more grazing lights for normal ambiguity, more wavelengths for color ambiguity, more polarization states for separation ambiguity, more backlight exposures for transparent regions, or more views for geometric uncertainty.
		
		\item \textbf{Material Libraries and Search:} Captured materials can be stored in a database with metadata, thumbnails, rendered previews, spectra, physical measurements, and learned embeddings. Users can search by image, text, measured sample, color, render appearance, physical property, supplier, or quality-control result.
		
		\item \textbf{Process Feedback:} In a production setting, captured maps can be used not only to reject defective parts but to adjust upstream processes, such as light exposure, curing time, coating thickness, pigment mix, printer calibration, weaving tension, drying temperature, polishing, sanding, cutting, or sorting.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	These potential extensions underline the versatility and scalability of the scanner system, paving the way for future advancements and wider applications in material scanning and imaging.
	
	\section{Applications and Impact}
	The proposed device offers broad applicability spanning across various domains. Its primary function lies in digitizing real-world materials and computing the parameters that describe these materials based on the Disney Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function (BRDF), OpenPBR, MaterialX, or other physically based material models. \cite{DISNEY_BRDF,OPENPBR,MATERIALX} This ability to accurately capture and recreate the physical characteristics of a material digitally opens up new possibilities in fields such as computer graphics, virtual reality, augmented reality, industrial design, e-commerce, fashion, architecture, digital twins, simulation, manufacturing, and robotics.
	
	Moreover, the device's versatility extends beyond material digitization, making it an effective tool in various other fields. In the medical domain, it could potentially be used for skin scanning, hair scanning, tissue appearance analysis, moisture analysis, or detection of abnormalities. For industrial applications, the device could serve as an optical inspection tool for surfaces such as steel slabs, painted panels, fabrics, paper, printed packages, automotive parts, injection-molded plastics, coatings, glass, food products, or electronic assemblies, aiding in quality control and defect detection.
